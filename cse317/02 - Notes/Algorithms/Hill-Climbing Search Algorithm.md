## Starting Point and the Problem

When optimizing a function without maintaining a search tree, the most natural greedy instinct is: *"Look at all immediate neighbors, pick the one that gives the highest increase in value, and move there. Repeat until no neighbor is better."* This intuitive strategy is known as **Hill-Climbing Search** (or *Steepest-Ascent Hill-Climbing*).

---

## Developing the Idea

Hill-climbing is often compared to climbing Mount Everest in a thick fog with amnesia: you can only see the ground immediately beneath your feet, and you simply take a step in the steepest upward direction.

```
       [ Current State ] ──► Evaluate all neighbors
                                     │
                    ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
                    │                                 │
          Best neighbor > Current           No neighbor > Current
                    │                                 │
                    ▼                                 ▼
         [ Move to best neighbor ]             [ TERMINATE: Local Max ]
```

---

## Algorithm Specification

```python
def HILL_CLIMBING(problem):
    current = problem.INITIAL_STATE
    while True:
        neighbors = problem.NEIGHBORS(current)
        best_neighbor = max(neighbors, key=lambda s: problem.VALUE(s))
        
        # If no neighbor improves upon current state, terminate
        if problem.VALUE(best_neighbor) <= problem.VALUE(current):
            return current
            
        current = best_neighbor
```

*(Note: If minimizing cost/conflicts, replace `max` with `min` and `<=` with `>=` for Steepest-Descent).*

---

## Major Vulnerabilities of Pure Hill-Climbing

1. **Local Maxima:** The algorithm halts at the peak of a small hill, unable to cross a valley to reach the higher global peak.
2. **Plateaus / Shoulders:** The algorithm wanders aimlessly along a flat surface or terminates prematurely if sideways moves are disallowed.
3. **Ridges:** Because neighbors are typically generated by altering one variable at a time, the algorithm must zigzag repeatedly back and forth across the ridge, making negligible progress along the crest.

---

## Variations of Hill-Climbing

To overcome these vulnerabilities, several variants have been developed:

1. **Stochastic Hill-Climbing:**
   Chooses randomly from among all uphill moves, weighting the probability of selection by the steepness of the improvement. Can navigate rugged terrain better than greedy steepest-ascent.
2. **First-Choice Hill-Climbing:**
   Generates neighbors randomly one at a time and immediately accepts the **first neighbor that is strictly better** than the current state. Ideal when a state has thousands of neighbors and evaluating all of them is too slow.
3. **Random-Restart Hill-Climbing:**
   The ultimate complete extension:
   - Run hill-climbing from a randomly generated initial state until it terminates at a peak.
   - Save the peak value.
   - Generate a new random initial state and repeat.
   - Keep running restarts until the global optimum is found or a resource threshold is reached.

### Mathematical Completeness of Random-Restart Hill-Climbing
Let $p$ be the probability that a single hill-climbing run successfully finds the global optimum.
The probability of failing across $k$ independent restarts is:
$$P(\text{failure after } k \text{ runs}) = (1 - p)^k$$
As $k \to \infty$:
$$\lim_{k \to \infty} (1 - p)^k = 0 \implies P(\text{success}) \to 1$$
Thus, **Random-Restart Hill-Climbing is complete with probability 1**!
The expected number of restarts required to find the global optimum is $\frac{1}{p}$.

## Common Mistakes

- Allowing infinite loops on plateaus by accepting sideways moves without a strict step limit.
- Assuming hill-climbing remembers previously visited states (it is completely memoryless).
